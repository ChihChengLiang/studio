The starting question

If there is no real camera, do light and lens still matter?

They matter more. A photographer standing in a street is handed perspective, light and weather and chooses among them. In a drawing or a render nothing is handed to you. The height of the horizon, how fast things shrink into the distance, where the light comes from, how soft the shadows are and how blurred the background is are all decided, either by you or by a default.

Defaults are choices too. new PerspectiveCamera(50) in three.js is a 50° vertical field of view, roughly a 26 mm wide-angle lens on a full-frame camera. No lights means flat ambient. No grade means untreated video. That is a large part of why first renders look alike: they share the same unexamined camera.

In 2D the lens is still there, under different names. The table translates. Use the photographic word to state the intent, then pick the 2D or 3D technique that delivers it.

Method

How professionals work through a shot

Cinematographers, storyboard artists and art directors make decisions in roughly this order. The story question comes first, then the camera, light and colour that serve it. Asking "what is this shot about?" before "what lens?" is most of the craft.

  1. The beatWhat changes in this moment? One idea per shot.
  2. Whose momentPick the subject and the point of view: are we with Mira, with Otto, or watching both?
  3. Shot sizeHow close emotionally. Wide for context, close for feeling.
  4. Angle and heightThe power relationship between viewer and subject.
  5. Lens and distanceHow the space feels: stretched and immersive, or compressed and observed.
  6. FocusWhat is allowed to be sharp, and therefore important.
  7. LightA motivated source, its direction, hardness and contrast ratio.
  8. CompositionPlacement, lines, layers, negative space, the edges of the frame.
  9. Colour and finishPalette, grade, aspect ratio, grain. The "look" comes last.

Work from references

A lookbook or mood board of frames from films, paintings and photographs that already have the feeling. Art directors talk in references ("lit like a Hopper, framed like Ozu") because it is faster than describing light from scratch.

Plan with a shot list

A storyboard and a shot list written in this vocabulary: "MCU Mira, 85 mm, eye level, key camera-left, golden hour." The same words make good prompts and art-direction notes.

Think in sequences

A single frame rarely stands alone. Pros vary shot size from wide to close, keep screen direction consistent, and plan a colour script (Pixar's term) so palette shifts with the story.

Meet the cast

Mira (1.55 m, red coat: the one saturated accent), Otto (2.05 m, dark coat and hat), Pip (a kid in a yellow raincoat, running) and a few passers-by at the gate of a small town: a lamp-lined street, a stone arch, trees and distant hills. The beat: Mira meets Otto under the streetlamp.

Gallery 1 · Camera

Shot size: how close are we?

Shot size is emotional distance. Every step closer trades context for feeling. The names are standard across film, comics and storyboards.

Principle: size in frame equals importance. Cutting closer is how a scene intensifies. Jumping straight from ELS to CU is a jolt, which is sometimes exactly the point.

Gallery 2 · Camera

Height and angle: who has the power?

Where the camera stands vertically decides whether the viewer looks up to, down on or across at a character. It also decides how much ground and how much sky end up in the frame.

Principle: looking up confers power and looking down takes it away. Eye level is the neutral contract with the audience. Anything else is a statement, so use it when the story means it.

Gallery 3 · Camera

Staging two people: relationship shots

With two characters, the camera takes sides. Who faces us, who is nearer the lens, whose shoulder we stand behind: each choice tells the audience whose story this is.

Principle: the 180° rule. Keep the camera on one side of the line between two characters, so Mira always looks right and Otto always looks left. Crossing the line flips screen direction and confuses the space, unless the confusion is the point.

Gallery 4 · Lens

Focal length: the same framing, a different world

Each tile frames Mira at the same size. Only the lens changes, so the camera has to move closer or farther to keep her that size. Perspective comes from distance; the lens just crops. Watch the background shrink with a wide lens and swell with a long one.

Try it: open any tile in the shot builder and drag the focal length. Framing is held, so the camera dollies while it zooms. That is the dolly zoom from Hitchcock's Vertigo and Spielberg's Jaws: the subject stays put while the world warps around them.

Gallery 5 · Lens

Depth of field: what gets to be sharp

A wide aperture (small f-number) gives shallow focus, and a narrow one gives deep focus. Sharpness is a pointer: the eye goes to the sharpest thing in the frame first.

Small number, big hole. The f-number is a ratio: focal length ÷ aperture diameter. On an 85 mm lens, f/1.4 is an opening of about 61 mm and f/11 only about 8 mm. Each full stop (1.4, 2, 2.8, 4, 5.6, 8, 11, 16, 22) halves the light, so f/11 lets in about 1/64 as much as f/1.4.

"Shallow" is about the sharp zone, not the blur. Picture the in-focus region as a slab of space at the focus distance. Shallow depth of field means a thin slab, so more of the scene in front and behind falls outside it and blurs. Deep depth of field means a thick slab that holds nearly everything. Three things make it shallower: a smaller f-number, a closer subject, and a longer lens.

Principle: shallow focus isolates and prettifies, but it also removes the world, and with it the context. Deep focus (Gregg Toland in Citizen Kane) lets action happen in several planes at once and trusts the viewer to look around.

Gallery 6 · Light

Key light direction: where the shadow falls

A studio close-up of Mira with one key light and a weak fill. Direction is described from the camera's point of view: front, three-quarter, side, back, below, above.

Principle: the side the shadow falls on is the side the audience reads as hidden. Front light reveals, side light sculpts, back light separates, and light from below disturbs because it never happens in nature.

Gallery 7 · Light

Quality, contrast and colour of light

The same face, now changing how big the source is (hard or soft), how much fill lifts the shadows (the key-to-fill ratio), and the colour temperature of each light.

Principle: soft light is kind and hard light is honest. High key (bright, low contrast) reads as open. Low key (dark, high contrast) reads as drama. Temperature contrast between a warm key and a cool fill adds depth without adding brightness.

Gallery 8 · Light

Time of day and motivated light

Outdoors the sun or a lamp is the key. Motivated light means every light appears to come from something in the world, so stylised light still feels believable.

Principle: a practical is a light that appears in the frame (the streetlamp, the windows). Practicals let you light a night scene with intent while the audience believes it is lit by the street.

Gallery 9 · Frame

Composition: arranging attention

Placement, lines, frames, layers and empty space. Guide overlays show the structure each tile is built on.

Principle: the rule of thirds is a starting habit, not a law. What matters is whether the arrangement agrees with the beat: centred for confrontation and formality, off-centre for movement and story, cramped for unease, empty for loneliness.

Gallery 10 · Time

Shutter: freezing or smearing time

Pip runs across the street at 5 m/s. Each blurred tile averages 18 sub-frames over a 1/15 s exposure, the way a real shutter integrates light.

Principle: blur is how a still image shows time. Cartier-Bresson's decisive moment freezes the peak of an action. A long exposure turns a crowd into ghosts and leaves only what stood still.

Gallery 11 · Frame

Aspect ratio: the shape of the canvas

The proportions of the frame decide what can sit side by side. Each tile is letterboxed inside the same 3:2 box so the shapes compare directly.

Gallery 12 · Finish

Colour grade: the same light, a different memory

The grade is applied after tone mapping, like a film stock or a LUT. It cannot fix lighting, but it sets the emotional temperature of everything at once.

Principle: restraint makes colour meaningful. Mira's red coat is the only saturated thing on this street, so in every grade that keeps colour, she is where the eye lands.

Gallery 13 · Air

Atmosphere: depth you can breathe

Haze lightens, cools and flattens things in proportion to their distance. Painters call it atmospheric or aerial perspective. It separates the planes of a picture without any lighting change.

General principles

The ideas underneath every choice

The galleries are vocabulary. These are the grammar: the reasons the vocabulary works, which carry over to any medium or style.

1 · Attention is the currency

The eye goes first to faces and eyes, then to the strongest value contrast, the sharpest area, the most saturated colour, the isolated element, and anything implying motion. Every tool on this page moves attention. When all of them point at the same subject the frame reads instantly. When they disagree you get tension, or confusion.

2 · Size and distance are intimacy

Bigger in frame means more important. Closer means more emotional, farther means more context, fate or loneliness. The camera's distance is the audience's relationship to the character.

3 · Height is power

Looking up makes a figure dominant. Looking down makes it vulnerable. Level means equal. Small shifts (5–10°) work below conscious notice, which is usually what you want.

4 · Stability vs tension

Horizontals and verticals calm the frame and symmetry formalises it. Diagonals add energy, a tilted horizon adds anxiety, and a cramped edge (short-siding, tight headroom) is claustrophobic. Space ahead of a gaze or motion gives relief.

5 · Separation

The subject must separate from what is behind it by at least one of: value (light against dark), colour (hue, saturation, temperature), focus, an edge light, or atmosphere. To check, squint until the image blurs and see whether the subject still reads.

6 · Light needs a motive

Audiences accept extreme stylisation as long as the light seems to come from somewhere: a window, a lamp, a screen, the sun. Decide the source first, then exaggerate it. Unmotivated light is what makes CG look like CG.

7 · Contrast sets the mood

The ratio between lit and shadowed sides is an emotional dial. At 1:1 everything is visible and nothing is hidden. At 16:1 half the story is in the dark. Hard edges feel truthful and tense; soft edges feel gentle and flattering.

8 · Colour means through restraint

A muted world with one accent (Mira's coat) directs the eye better than a rainbow. Use warm vs cool for depth (warm advances, cool recedes), and give each act or mood its own palette.

9 · Build depth in planes

Foreground, middle ground, background. Depth comes from overlap, diminishing size, converging lines, focus falloff, value falloff and atmospheric perspective. Stack several and even a flat illustration feels deep.

10 · Simplify

Everything in the frame costs attention. Ask what you can remove: a prop, a light, a colour, a second subject. Pros are defined less by what they add than by what they leave out.

11 · Shots mean in sequence

The Kuleshov effect: the same neutral face reads as hungry, grieving or loving depending on the shot cut next to it. In comics, storyboards and games, design shots as a progression: establish, approach, reveal, react.

12 · Break rules on purpose

Every rule here is a default that serves clarity. Breaking one is a signal: a centred face for confrontation, a crossed 180° line for disorientation, a blown-out exposure for memory or heat. Break it once and clearly, so it reads as a choice.

Inspiration

Genres as bundles of choices

A genre or a personal style is a consistent set of the choices above. The sketches below re-dress the same street. The list after them names more traditions worth studying, with the choices that define each.

Film noir

Hard low-key light, venetian-blind shadows, wet streets, low angles, Dutch tilts, black and white.

Look at: The Third Man (1949), Double Indemnity (1944).

Western

Extreme long shots of landscape, scope ratio, low horizons, hard sun, then extreme close-ups on eyes in a standoff.

Look at: Sergio Leone's Once Upon a Time in the West, John Ford's The Searchers.

Horror

Negative space where something could appear, wide lenses close to faces, under-light, darkness that hides more than it shows, slow push-ins.

Look at: The Shining (1980), Hereditary (2018).

Romance and melodrama

Soft backlight, shallow focus, warm grade, close two-shots, frames within frames that trap lovers apart.

Look at: Wong Kar-wai's In the Mood for Love (shot by Christopher Doyle and Mark Lee Ping-bing).

Symmetric tableau

Centred, planimetric framing (camera square to a wall), flat even light, curated pastel palettes, characters arranged like a diorama.

Look at: Wes Anderson's The Grand Budapest Hotel; Kubrick's one-point corridors.

Street and documentary

28–35 mm at eye level, available light, deep focus, grain, the decisive moment, layered city scenes.

Look at: Henri Cartier-Bresson; Saul Leiter (colour, frames through glass and snow); Fan Ho (light and shadow geometry in 1950s–60s Hong Kong).

Sci-fi epic

Tiny figures against huge structures, scope ratio, monochrome palettes, haze and silhouette, symmetrical architecture.

Look at: Blade Runner 2049 (Roger Deakins), Dune (2021, Greig Fraser).

Neon night

Coloured practicals as key lights, magenta and cyan, rain and reflections, shallow-focus bokeh.

Look at: Blade Runner (1982), Chungking Express (1994).

Anime and painted backgrounds

Big skies with cumulus clouds, low horizons, layered painted planes (the multiplane camera), calm wide shots that hold on the place, lens flares.

Look at: Studio Ghibli's My Neighbor Totoro; Makoto Shinkai's Your Name.

Ozu style

Low static camera near the height of someone seated on the floor, frontal compositions, "pillow shots" of empty places between scenes.

Look at: Yasujirō Ozu's Tokyo Story (1953).

Tenebrism and chiaroscuro

One hard source, most of the canvas in near-black, figures emerging from darkness.

Look at: Caravaggio's The Calling of Saint Matthew; Rembrandt's self-portraits; Georges de La Tour's candlelit scenes.

Quiet interiors and isolation

Soft window light, figures seen from behind, empty rooms, or hard sunlight and the viewer as a voyeur through a window.

Look at: Vilhelm Hammershøi's interiors; Edward Hopper's Nighthawks and Morning Sun.

Staged tableau photography

Single frames lit like film sets, with every lamp placed; suburban scenes made uncanny.

Look at: Gregory Crewdson; Jeff Wall; Cindy Sherman's Untitled Film Stills.

Isometric and game views

Orthographic camera, fixed angle near 30–35° down, readable silhouettes, no depth of field, high-key and saturated.

Look at: Monument Valley; Hades; classic SimCity.

Fashion and editorial

Hard on-camera flash, bold flat colour, wide lenses low on full figures, strong poses, high contrast.

Look at: Guy Bourdin; Juergen Teller; Tim Walker for the opposite, lavish staging.

Product and still life

Large soft sources, graduated backgrounds, designed specular highlights, perfect focus front to back.

Look at: Irving Penn's still lifes; the Dutch Golden Age pronkstilleven.
