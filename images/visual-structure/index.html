Shape and visual structure

Every picture is built from a few basic parts: shape, line, tone, color, space, movement and rhythm. Each part can be kept similar across the frame (affinity) or set against itself (contrast). Contrast raises the intensity the viewer feels, and affinity lowers it. This page shows each part, what it tends to mean, and how to plan it across a story. It follows Bruce Block's The Visual Story, with help from Molly Bang's Picture This.

The one principle

Contrast and affinity

Bruce Block's observation is that every picture, whatever its subject, is made of the same few visual components, and each component can be organized in one of two ways.

Affinity means the component stays similar throughout: all round shapes, all horizontal lines, all middle values. It reads as calm and unified, sometimes dull. Contrast means it's set against itself: round against pointed, horizontal against diagonal, black against white. It reads as tense and energetic, sometimes chaotic.

The useful part is that this is a dial, not a style. If a scene should feel calm, keep most components in affinity, and the audience will feel the calm without being able to say why. As the story builds, push components toward contrast. Contrast is also relative: after a long calm stretch, even a small contrast feels big.

It works at three scales: inside one frame, between one shot and the next, and across a whole sequence.

ComponentWhat it isAffinityContrast

ToneBrightness (value), regardless of hueA narrow range of valuesThe full range; darkest beside lightest

Why it helps in 2D and 3D. Each component is a knob you control directly. In 2D you draw it. In 3D you set it through modeling, layout, lens and light. Once each knob has a name, you can decide its setting before you start, instead of hoping the mood shows up. Color has its own page: Color lessons from painters. Gallery 1 · Shape

Circle, square, triangle: the three shape families

Shape language is the idea that simple geometric shapes carry feelings, mostly because of how they would behave as physical objects. Circles roll and have no edges. Squares sit still and resist being pushed. Triangles point and cut. Character designers build each character from one dominant family, and production designers do the same with whole worlds.

A tendency, not a law. Shape language works because viewers have expectations, which also means you can betray them on purpose. Most good designs mix families with one clearly in charge: a round character with a square jaw is friendly but stubborn.

Gallery 2 · Shape

Shape across a world: environments, size and silhouette

The same families scale up to whole environments, and contrast between a character's shape and its world is one of the strongest ways to make the character stand out. Two more shape habits matter as much as the families: varying sizes, and keeping silhouettes readable.

Two quick tests. The silhouette test: fill the character or the whole composition with black. If you can still read the action and the mood, the shapes are doing the work. The big, medium, small test: can you point to one dominant shape, a secondary one and a scatter of small ones? Equal sizes spread attention evenly, so nothing leads.

Gallery 3 · Line

Line: direction carries feeling

A line can be drawn (an edge, a horizon, a tree trunk) or implied (a gaze, a gesture, a row of things, the path of something moving). Direction matters most: horizontal rests, vertical stands, diagonal moves. Curved lines feel soft and organic, straight ones built and rigid. With affinity of line, one direction rules. With contrast, directions fight.

Gallery 4 · Tone

Tone: brightness alone

Tone is how light or dark something is, whatever its hue. It pulls the eye harder than anything else, and it's what still reads from across a room. These tiles are greyscale to keep color out of the way. Color lessons covers value structure in color. Here the question is contrast and affinity: how much of the range you use, and where you put the extremes.

The squint test. Squint at your picture, or blur it, until detail disappears. What's left is the tonal structure. If your subject vanishes into its surroundings, no amount of detail will rescue it. Fix the values first.

Gallery 5 · Space · three.js

Space: building depth one cue at a time

A frame is flat, so depth is always an illusion built from cues. You can add the cues one at a time, and each tile below adds one to the tile before it. Strip them all away and you get flat space: graphic, formal, airless. Add them all and you get deep space: immersive and real, sometimes overwhelming.

The four kinds of space

Block names four. Deep and flat are the two ends. Limited space keeps every surface square to the camera, like theatre flats, but allows overlap and size change, so you get some depth and no diagonals. Ambiguous space removes the clues that tell you size and distance, so the viewer can't work out how big things are or where they sit.

In 3D, depth comes almost free. A perspective camera gives you size change and converging lines, lights give you shading, and fog gives you aerial perspective. Flatness is the choice that takes effort: a long lens or an orthographic camera, surfaces square to the camera, flat light. Contrast of space also works across a cut. Many films keep a calm stretch flat and go deep when the world opens up, or the reverse.

Gallery 6 · Movement · animated

Movement: objects, camera and the eye

Block separates three kinds of movement: actual movement of objects in the frame, apparent movement caused by the camera, and induced movement of the viewer's eye across a still image. Direction (horizontal, vertical, diagonal, toward or away), speed and quality (smooth or jerky) each carry meaning. With contrast, directions oppose each other, or one thing moves while everything else holds still.

Still images move too. Induced movement is the main tool for 2D art: the eye goes first to the strongest contrast, then follows lines, gazes, size steps and repeated shapes. Faint trails are drawn on these tiles so the direction still reads if animation is paused.

Gallery 7 · Rhythm

Rhythm: repetition, alternation and tempo

Rhythm comes from repeated elements and the intervals between them. In a still image that means fence posts, windows, trees, figures. In film it also means the timing of movement and the length of shots. Regular rhythm feels calm and predictable. Irregular rhythm feels lively or nervous. When a rhythm breaks, the eye goes to the break.

Gallery 9 · Feelings

Feelings from paper shapes: Molly Bang's principles

In Picture This (1991), the illustrator Molly Bang retold Little Red Riding Hood in cut paper: triangles, rectangles and a few colors. With no faces to read, the book shows how much feeling comes from shape, size, position and color alone. Her principles, paraphrased:

  • Horizontal shapes feel stable and calm. Vertical shapes feel active, reaching up. Diagonals feel like motion and tension.
  • The upper half of a picture feels free, happy or powerful. The lower half feels heavier and more grounded, sometimes sad or threatened.
  • The center of the picture pulls attention most strongly. The edges feel like the edges of the world.
  • Pointed shapes frighten. Rounded shapes feel safe and comforting.
  • Larger things feel stronger.
  • We notice contrast. Whatever differs from its surroundings stands out.
  • Light backgrounds feel safe. Dark backgrounds feel frightening, because we can't see what's in them.
  • We link things by color more strongly than by shape.

Intensity

Planning

Plan intensity across a story

Block's practical advice: before drawing anything, graph how intense the story should feel at each moment, then assign visual contrast to match. A classic shape rises through conflict to a climax and falls to a resolution. Below is a six-beat story told only with the lab's dials: Pip flies a kite, the wind takes it, a chase, the climax at the town wall, home again. Notice which components carry each beat. The thick line is the average; the thin ones are the seven components.

Save the peak for the climax

If every frame is already at full contrast, the climax has nowhere to go. This is the most common mistake in student work: every image at maximum, so none of them registers.

Components don't have to move together

Let one or two components carry a story thread (color for a relationship, space for freedom) and keep the others quiet so the important one reads.

Contrast across the cut

Cutting from a calm frame to an intense one is itself a contrast. Cutting between similar frames is affinity, and feels smooth. Editing is where this becomes a tool of its own.

Progression

A component that changes steadily over a sequence (frames growing darker, shapes growing sharper, space growing deeper) tells the audience something is building, even before the plot says so.

Use it

Turning the vocabulary into 2D and 3D art

ComponentIn 2DIn 3D
ShapeThumbnail in silhouette. Build each character from one dominant primitive and repeat that family in its props and home.Block out with primitives first. Carry the dominant family through architecture and props. Check silhouettes from the actual shot camera.
LineDraw the line of action before the figure. Block the composition as three to five lines.Layout lines: horizon, walls, road edges. Camera roll for diagonals. Lean props. Pose rigs along a curve.
TonePlan in three values, at postage-stamp size, before any color. Squint test.Render a grey clay pass. Light to set value groups. Exposure and fog compress or widen the range.
ColorA limited palette with one accent. Group by hue family.Albedo palette, light temperature, color grade.
SpaceOverlap, size steps, diagonals and haze for depth. Frontal planes and even sizes for flatness.Lens and camera height, fog, layout in depth. Orthographic or long lens to flatten.
MovementGaze and gesture directions, motion lines, reading order.Animation paths, camera moves, parallax between layers.
RhythmRepeat elements with variation; leave rests.Instancing with jitter, prop spacing, animation and shot timing.
  1. Graph firstSketch the story's intensity curve and mark where each component should peak.
  2. Assign shapesOne shape family for the world, one for the hero, one for the threat.
  3. Three valuesTiny thumbnails in three values, no color. Pick the one that reads instantly.
  4. Vary one dialMake six versions of one image changing a single component, as the galleries here do. It's grid search applied to structure.
  5. TestSilhouette test (fill black), squint test (blur), and a flip test (mirror it to see it fresh).
  6. Master studyTake a frame you love and diagram it: shape family, dominant line, value groups, depth cues. Then say where the contrast is.
