Secrets in the overlap

Visual cryptography splits a picture into sheets that each look like pure noise. Stack the right sheets and the picture appears, decoded by the eye with no computer at all. For an artist that is a set of strange, useful elements: images that exist only in an overlap, reveals that need several people, pictures that change when a sheet is turned or flipped, and noise that is quietly full of meaning. Everything below is drawn live from real schemes, and every number under a tile is measured from the pixels.

1 · The trick

Two sheets of noise, one picture

Naor and Shamir's 1994 scheme splits every pixel of a black-and-white secret into a 2×2 block on each of two sheets. Every block has exactly two dark subpixels, whichever pixel it came from, so one sheet alone carries no information at all, not just very little. Stacking the sheets is a logical OR: wherever either sheet is dark, light is blocked. Drag the transparency onto the paper.

The codebook

one of 6 patterns, picked at random per pixel

White pixel: both sheets get the same random pattern, so the stack has 2 of 4 subpixels dark and reads as grey.

Black pixel: the second sheet gets the complement, so the stack is 4 of 4 dark and reads as black.

Two costs come with it. Pixel expansion: each pixel became 4 subpixels. Contrast loss: white comes back only half white, a relative contrast α of ½. Almost every later scheme is a different trade between these two costs and what the sheets look like.

2 · Basis matrices

The cost of more people

The general recipe for n sheets, all needed, is a pair of basis matrices, one row per sheet. The white matrix lists every even-sized subset of the sheets as a column, and the black matrix every odd-sized one. Each pixel shuffles the columns, and row i becomes sheet i's block. Any n−1 rows look identical in both matrices, so nothing leaks. Stacking all n leaves exactly one white column for a white pixel and none for a black one. The cost is that each pixel needs 2ⁿ⁻¹ subpixels and contrast falls to 1/2ⁿ⁻¹. Add people and the secret fades.

3 · Threshold (k, n)

Any k of five cards

Threshold schemes relax "all n" to "any k of n". Fewer than k sheets are provably blank, and k or more show the image. Toggle the cards below. The basis matrices here were found by a small search over matrices built from all columns of a given weight. For (2,5) that search reaches the known optimum, α = 0.3; for larger k it is simply the best this construction found. The image gets fainter as k rises. Average the blocks to see what a camera or scanner would recover from the same stack.

4 · Meaningful shares

A bird, a cage, and a key between them

Noise sheets announce that they hide something. Extended visual cryptography (Ateniese and colleagues) gives each sheet its own cover picture: a cover-black pixel gets 3 of 4 dark subpixels and a cover-white one gets 2, while the stack still decodes the secret. The cost is contrast on both sides. Below is a grid search over four schemes. Each row shows share 1, share 2 and the stack, with both contrasts measured. In the random-grid rows, one dial λ sets how much of each sheet is cover. Watch the covers bleed into the stack as λ rises: the survey calls this inter-share interference, and it is also a lovely texture.

Grid search as a habit. Fix the secret, then vary one thing per row and one per column. Here the rows are schemes and the columns are what each viewer sees. The same layout works for any art parameter: palettes against compositions, brushes against subjects.

5 · Progressive

A picture that arrives in layers

Threshold schemes are all-or-nothing. Progressive schemes let clarity grow with every sheet added. This demo uses a simple probabilistic construction, written for this page and not taken from the papers. For a black pixel, one random sheet of six gets a dark dot. For a white pixel, all six get a dark dot with probability 1/6, and otherwise none do. Every sheet is therefore 1/6 dark wherever you look, so one sheet shows nothing. Each added sheet covers more of the black pixels while the white ones stay as they were, so contrast climbs to (t−1)/6.

6 · Dither as medium

Grey secrets go through a halftone first

Classic schemes only share black and white, so a grey picture is dithered first, and the dither becomes the grain of the reveal. The top row shows four dithers of the same shaded sphere (the same family as the halftone notebook). The bottom row shows each one shared with a random grid and stacked. Halftone visual cryptography (Zhou, Arce and Di Crescenzo) takes this further by weaving the secret into the halftone cells of grey covers.

7 · Multi-secret

Rotate, flip, fold

One pair of sheets can hold several secrets if each secret gets its own slice of every cell, and the second sheet is built to line up with the first only after a given move. Wu and Chen's circular shares give a different secret at each rotation angle. Flip visual cryptography gives a second secret when one sheet is turned over. Tagged visual cryptography stamps a tag that appears when a single sheet is folded onto itself. In each panel, a secret appears only at the exact move. With a quarter of the contrast left, the pictures are faint; Squint blurs the panel the way half-closed eyes do.

Turntable

Flip

Stacked face up, the sheets show a kite. Flipped over, the same top sheet shows a bird.

Fold (tagged share)

One sheet, folded onto itself, shows its tag: a star. It still works as a normal share for some other secret.

How the slices work. Every cell is split into strips that a move does not mix up: radial rings for a rotation, top and bottom halves for a left-right flip or fold. Strip j of the second sheet is computed as an XOR against the strip of the first sheet that it will land on after move j. In any other position the strips meet random partners and stay noise. Each extra secret costs contrast, because it takes its own slice of the cell.

8 · OR vs XOR

Stacked by light, or computed exactly

Physical stacking is OR: ink only adds, so a white pixel can never come back fully white, and half of it stays dark. A computer can use XOR instead, and XOR undoes itself: sheet 1 XOR sheet 2 gives back the secret bit for bit. The same trick works on whole grey bytes. The choice is aesthetic as well as technical: smoky, analog reveals or perfect digital ones.

9 · Verifiable

The forged share

Visual cryptography trusts every holder. A holder who has seen the real result can work out the other sheet and print a new one that stacks to anything, and it looks just as random as an honest sheet. Verifiable schemes add a check. In this simple version, the dealer keeps a verification sheet, the complement of the honest share. The honest share plus the verification sheet stacks to solid black. A forged share leaves white specks wherever it was changed, the same kind of tamper map the survey describes. This version catches only half of the changed pixels: those that went from dark to light.

10 · Typography

Letters as subpixels

Lin and colleagues' letter-based scheme replaces pixels with letters. This is a looser, art-first version: each cell is a letter, set reversed out of a dark block when the cell is dark and in plain ink otherwise. Each sheet reads as a page of text. Stacked, the dark blocks gather where the secret is.

11 · Lab

Make your own sheets

Pick a secret and a scheme, choose which sheets are in the stack, and drag on the stage to misalign the top sheet. Even a one-subpixel offset destroys the picture, which is why printed shares need registration marks. Save the sheets exports every share at print resolution with crop marks, ready for transparency film.

12 · Story

Four friends, four sheets, one kite

A progressive scheme maps directly onto a story beat. Pip and three friends each hold a sheet that looks like static. Each one who joins makes the picture a little clearer, so the reveal climbs the way an intensity curve does. Plan it with the story planner on the visual structure page: the last sheet lands on the climax.

Glossary and sources

TermMeaning
ShareOne sheet. Alone it reveals nothing about the secret.
Access structureWhich groups of shares may recover the secret (qualified) and which must learn nothing (forbidden).
Basis matricesTwo n × m 0/1 matrices, one for white pixels and one for black. Every pixel shuffles the columns of one of them, and row i becomes share i.
Pixel expansion mSubpixels per secret pixel on each share. (n, n) Naor–Shamir needs 2ⁿ⁻¹.
Relative contrast αHow much lighter a recovered white pixel is than a recovered black one, from 0 to 1. Classic (2,2) gives ½.
Random gridKafri and Keren's 1987 scheme with no pixel expansion: share 2 is share 1, inverted wherever the secret is black.
Probabilistic / size-invariantPick one random column of the basis matrix per pixel instead of all of them, so the shares keep the secret's size and contrast holds on average.
Extended (EVC)Shares carry meaningful cover images while the stack still decodes the secret.
Halftone VCGrey covers are halftoned and the secret is woven into the halftone cells.
ProgressiveClarity grows with the number of shares stacked, instead of a hard threshold.
VerifiableShares can be checked for tampering before being trusted.
Multi-secretOne set of shares holds several secrets, read by rotating, flipping or folding.
XOR-basedRecovered by computation instead of stacking. Exact, including grey and color.
Recursive / learning-basedSecrets nested inside the shares of larger secrets, and shares generated by trained networks. Covered by the surveys, not shown here.
