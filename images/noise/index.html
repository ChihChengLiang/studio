Randomness with a shape

Ken Perlin built his noise in 1983 so that computer-generated surfaces would stop looking machine-made. The trick was not randomness but controlled randomness: noise whose detail lives at one chosen size. Stack it at several sizes and you can dial how rough a thing is, which turns out to be how nature varies too. This page measures that with a Fourier transform at every step, then puts noise to work beyond terrain: camera shake, wandering, flowing particles, marble and smoke, and loops with no seam.

1 · Perlin, 1985 and 2002

What makes it Perlin

White noise is an independent random value per pixel: every frequency equally, which looks like TV static and gives you nothing to design with. Value noise puts random values on a grid and smooths between them; better, but it keeps a lot of energy at the lowest frequencies and shows the grid. Gradient noise, Perlin's idea, puts random slopes on the grid instead, so every grid point has value zero and the bumps sit between them. That pushes the energy into one band, about one cycle per grid cell: the noise has a size.

The bottom row is each pattern's power spectrum: the centre is coarse, the edges are fine, brighter means more energy. Square-grid noises burn a faint cross into the spectrum: leftover fine detail lined up with the grid axes, which the eye reads as a grid. Under each tile, "fine detail" is the share of energy above twice the lattice frequency, and its axis/diagonal ratio says how strongly that detail follows the grid (1 = no preference). Perlin's 2002 blend curve (6t⁵ − 15t⁴ + 10t³ instead of 3t² − 2t³) has no kink in its slope at cell edges, so it leaves less fine detail behind. Simplex noise works on a triangular lattice and has the weakest cross.

2 · Mandelbrot and Van Ness, 1968 · Voss, 1985

fBm and why it looks natural

Fractional Brownian motion (fBm) adds octaves of noise: each one lacunarity times finer and gain times fainter. Because each octave has a size, the sum has a power spectrum that falls as a power law across the octaves it covers, with a slope set by the Hurst exponent H = log(1/gain) / log(lacunarity). For a surface, power ∝ f−(2H+2) and the fractal dimension is D = 3 − H. Gain 0.5 with lacunarity 2 gives H = 1, slope −4, a smooth hillside; raise the gain and the detail survives into the fine scales.

Photographs of natural scenes have power spectra close to f−2 (Field 1987; van der Schaaf and van Hateren 1996), and visual neurons seem tuned to exactly that statistic. So "looks natural" has a measurable meaning: the right spectral slope. The chart fits the slope of the image on the left.

3 · Eiserloh, 2016 · Reynolds, 1999 · Bridson, 2007

Noise as motion

Sample noise along time instead of space and you get a value that wanders smoothly. Camera shake as Squirrel Eiserloh described it: keep a trauma value that jumps when something hits and decays steadily; shake by trauma² times noise. The square makes small hits subtle and big hits violent; the noise makes the motion continuous, so it reads as a physical jolt. The right panel uses a fresh random offset every frame instead. The measurement is how much of the shake's energy is above 10 Hz.

Wander. Three agents with the same speed, tuned so their paths are similarly winding. One turns by a random walk, one by Craig Reynolds' wander circle (a target point jittered around a circle ahead of it), one by 1D noise. Straightness is net distance over path length across the last 8 seconds; jitter is how abruptly the turn rate changes.

Curl flow. Treat a noise field as a stream function and move particles along its contours (the curl). Such a flow has no sources or sinks, so particles swirl forever without bunching up, which is what makes it look like smoke or water. Moving them along the slope of the same field instead (the gradient) drains them into a few sinks. The measurement is how uneven the particle density gets, against the unevenness of pure chance.

4 · Quilez

Domain warping

Instead of reading noise at a point p, read it at p moved by more noise: f(p + k·q(p)). Do it twice, f(p + k·r(p + k·q(p))), and the pattern looks as if it has been stirred. Inigo Quilez popularised the double warp; marble (a sine bent by turbulence) and wood (rings bent by noise) are older versions of the same idea. The right panel shows the first warp as arrows over the unwarped noise.

Warping changes the texture's statistics in a measurable way: it bends structure. The coherence below is computed from the image's local gradients: 1 means perfectly parallel stripes in every neighbourhood, 0 means no preferred direction. Marble and the gas giant start as straight stripes (1.00 with no warp), and the stronger the warp, the more their stripes turn within a short distance, which is what reads as flowing. The double warp folds plain fBm the same way.

5 · Periodic noise

Seamless loops

Running noise forward in time never comes back to where it started, so a "loop" made by cutting it jumps at the cut. Two fixes. For a single value over time, walk around a circle through 2D noise: after one lap you are exactly where you began, and the speed is constant. For images, wrap the lattice: if the random gradients repeat every P cells, so does the noise. Wrap the time axis and the animation loops; wrap x and y and the texture tiles.

Glossary and sources

TermMeaning
LatticeThe integer grid where noise stores its random values or gradients.
Value / gradient noiseRandom values at lattice points, smoothed; or random slopes at lattice points, so the value there is zero.
Band-limitedWith its energy concentrated around one frequency, so it has a characteristic size.
Power spectrumHow much variance an image has at each spatial frequency.
fBmFractional Brownian motion: a sum of octaves, giving a power-law spectrum.
Lacunarity, gainFrequency ratio and amplitude ratio between successive octaves.
Hurst exponent HRoughness parameter: H = log(1/gain) / log(lacunarity). Smaller H is rougher.
Fractal dimension D3 − H for a surface, 2 − H for a profile. 1 is a smooth line, 2 fills the plane.
Curl noiseA velocity field taken as the curl of noise: divergence-free, so it swirls without sources or sinks.
Domain warpingEvaluating noise at coordinates displaced by noise.
Periodic noiseNoise whose lattice repeats, so the pattern tiles in space or loops in time.
