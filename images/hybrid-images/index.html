Two pictures in one

A hybrid image shows one picture up close and a different one from across the room. It works because the visual system does not see an image whole: it splits it into channels by how fine the detail is, and which channels you can use depends on how far away you stand. This page measures your own sensitivity, takes pictures apart into those channels, builds hybrids and predicts the distance at which each one flips. All pictures are drawn on the page or come from this notebook's sprite sheets, and you can load your own.

1 · Campbell and Robson, 1968

Your contrast sensitivity

The chart below is one image. Stripes get finer from left to right, and their contrast falls from bottom to top. The contrast is exactly the same along every row, yet the stripes seem to reach higher in the middle than at either side: where they fade is your own contrast sensitivity function, drawn by your eye. It is band-pass. You are most sensitive at a few cycles per degree of visual angle and much less to very coarse or very fine patterns.

Neurons in primary visual cortex are tuned to narrow ranges of stripe width, so the visual system works like a bank of spatial-frequency filters, much as the cochlea is a bank of sound-frequency filters (the consonance page). The curve is the envelope of those channels. To put real units on the axis, match the dashed box to a bank card and set your viewing distance. Then click where the stripes vanish at a few points to trace your curve.

2 · The Laplacian pyramid

An image in bands

Blur an image a little, then a bit more, then more again, and take the difference between each blur and the next. Every difference holds one octave of detail, from edges and texture down to large patches of light and dark, and adding all the differences back gives the original exactly. Burt and Adelson called this a Laplacian pyramid. It is a reasonable sketch of how the early visual system holds an image.

The coarse bands carry layout, value structure and mood. The fine bands carry edges, texture and identity details. Schyns and Oliva showed that people take in the coarse layer first, within about 50 ms, and fill in the fine layer after. Squinting is a low-pass filter: it keeps the bottom rows of this table and drops the top ones, which is why painters squint to judge masses and values. Untick bands to rebuild the picture from any subset.

3 · Oliva, Torralba and Schyns, 2006

Make a hybrid

A hybrid is the low-pass of one picture added to the high-pass of another. Close up, the high-pass detail sits near the peak of your sensitivity and wins; the blurred picture reads as shading. Step back and the whole image shrinks on your retina, so every stripe in it gets finer in cycles per degree: the detail slides off the fine end of your curve, and the blurred picture slides into the sensitive range. The pyramid strip shows the same hybrid at halving sizes, which is the same as standing twice as far away each time.

The spectrum plot shows the two pictures' energy per frequency band and your eye's window for the chosen distance. The prediction weights each band by the model sensitivity and reports which picture should dominate, and where they swap. It is a model of an average observer, so treat the switch distance as an estimate and check it with the pyramid.

4 · Harmon and Julesz, 1973

The blocky portrait

Average a picture over coarse blocks and it becomes hard to recognise, even though all the coarse information is still there. Harmon and Julesz showed why: the sharp block edges add high-frequency noise, and noise in the band just above the picture's own frequencies masks it, because both land in neighbouring channels. Remove that adjacent band and the picture comes back; remove a band far above it and nothing changes. Distance and squinting do the first for free, which is the trick behind Dalí's Lincoln and every pixel-art portrait that only reads from across the room.

Glossary and sources

TermMeaning
Spatial frequencyHow fine a pattern is: stripe pairs per unit. Per image (cycles per image) is a property of the picture; per degree of visual angle (cpd) depends on how big it looks.
ContrastMichelson contrast, (Lmax − Lmin) / (Lmax + Lmin), in light, not in pixel values.
CSFContrast sensitivity function: 1 / the faintest visible contrast, per spatial frequency. Band-pass, peaking at a few cpd.
ChannelA population of visual neurons tuned to about one octave of spatial frequency and a range of orientations.
Low-pass / high-passKeep only the coarse / only the fine part of an image. Here both use a Gaussian blur; the cutoff is where the blur halves a stripe's contrast.
Laplacian pyramidAn image stored as differences of successive blurs, one octave each, plus a coarse remainder. Sums back to the original exactly.
Hybrid imageLow-pass of one picture plus high-pass of another, so which one you see depends on distance.
Critical-band maskingNoise hides a pattern most when it is close to the pattern in frequency, as in hearing.
