The starting point

What to call the dotted style

The reference was the Taiwan Spectrum poster (National Museum of Modern Art Tainan, 2026–27): lettering and shapes built from a coarse grid of round dots in four flat inks. The general name for that look is halftone. The related terms below are the ones worth searching for.

Halftone (AM)
Dots sit on a fixed grid and their size carries the tone. This is the newspaper and screen-print method, and what the poster uses.
Stochastic (FM)
Every dot is the same size and their density carries the tone. Inkjet printers work this way, and error diffusion is the usual algorithm.
Dot matrix, dot grid
The strict grid also recalls LED signboards and dot-matrix printers.
Overprint, misregistration
Colour layers printed separately and slightly out of line, as in risograph and screen printing.
Ben-Day dots
Flat, bright colour dots in the style of Roy Lichtenstein's comic panels.
Ishihara style
Letters hidden in fields of coloured dots, like a colour-blindness test plate. A good fit for an exhibition called Spectrum.

Search terms: halftone poster, dot grid typography, Ishihara-inspired graphic design.

  • Gradient at 4× zoom. AM keeps an even rhythm and the dots swell until they merge into a solid area. FM looks like grain that thickens toward the dark end.
  • Angle. 45° is the traditional angle for black because the eye notices the grid least there. In four-colour printing each ink gets its own angle so the grids don't beat against each other and produce moiré.
  • Dot shape. Round dots jump in darkness around 50% grey, when they start touching. Ellipse dots smooth that jump. Lines give an engraved, banknote look.
  • Shapes with large cells. AM edges go jagged because detail can't be smaller than one cell. FM keeps edges crisper at the same dot size.

Shepard tone meets AM: an ever-brightening field

A Shepard tone stacks tones an octave apart that all rise together. Each fades in at the bottom and out at the top, so the scale seems to climb forever. Here, several dot grids each twice as fine as the next shrink continuously and fade out as they get finer, while a new coarse grid fades in.

Envelope: each layer's ink across its life, from coarse to fine

  • Pitch becomes grid frequency. Each layer's cell size halves over its life, so the grid keeps getting finer by a factor of 2 per octave.
  • Loudness becomes ink coverage. The bell-shaped envelope means a layer never appears or vanishes abruptly. The dots on the curve are the layers.
  • Why it works. Every dot is always shrinking, which the eye reads as "getting lighter", while the mean ink stays in a narrow band.
  • Fade-in length is the seam. Short values strengthen the effect but let you catch coarse dots blooming. Around 15–25% balances it. More layers smooth the mean-ink wobble.

The same trick on a shaded sphere

Each layer's ink is scaled by the sphere's tone at that point. The layers are weighted so that together they always add up to the right shade. The sphere keeps its shading while every dot keeps shrinking.

// per point: split the sphere's tone between layers by envelope weight
w[L] = env(phase[L]) / sum(env)           // weights add to 1
d[L] = 1 - (1 - tone) ** w[L]             // layer coverage; layers multiply back to tone
r[L] = cell[L] * sqrt(d[L] / PI)          // round-dot radius
  • The shadow side carries the illusion. Dark areas have big dots, so you can clearly watch them shrink. In the highlight the dots are already tiny.
  • Turn the light while it plays to stack a real lighting change on top of the illusory one.
  • Angle per layer hides the coarse layer's fade-in better on large flat-toned areas.

Demo 4

Sixteen ways to render one sphere in black and white

From simple thresholding to single-line TSP art. Change the light angle or pixel size and every tile re-renders.

225°
Pixel size
  • Threshold, random, blue noise. Threshold loses all the shading. Random keeps the tones but looks grainy. Blue noise keeps the tones with the cleanest grain because its dots are spread as evenly as possible.
  • Bayer vs blue noise. Both are lookup tables: fast and stable in animation. Bayer shows its grid; blue noise hides it.
  • Floyd–Steinberg, Atkinson, Riemersma. These spread each pixel's rounding error to its neighbours. Floyd–Steinberg leaves faint "worm" patterns in smooth mid-tones, Atkinson blows out highlights and deepens shadows, and Riemersma (error flowing along a Hilbert curve) has a more organic, swirly texture.
  • Line screen, crosshatch, spiral, squiggle. Strokes replace dots. Spiral and squiggle shift smoothly as you turn the light, since they're continuous lines and not a pixel grid.
  • Stippling vs TSP art. The same evenly spaced dots, but TSP joins them into one continuous line you could send to a pen plotter.

Demo 5

Sixteen ways to render a coloured sphere

Limited-palette dithering, print processes, screens, and painterly or poster-style dots, including a nod to the Taiwan Spectrum poster. Use the rainbow sphere to give the palettes more to work with.

225°
Pixel size
  • Dithering makes up for a small palette. Compare "8 colours, no dither" with the Floyd–Steinberg tile. Both use the same eight colours, but dithering brings back the orange and the shading.
  • Linear light matters. The linear-light tile spreads error in physical light values. Highlights come out brighter and mid-tones closer to the original. Simple dithering code often gets this subtly wrong.
  • RGB vs OKLab. Choosing the nearest palette colour by how the eye sees it (OKLab) usually picks better neighbours, especially in shadows and on the rainbow sphere's blues and purples.
  • Print vs screen. CMYK and riso subtract light, so overlaps get darker. RGB subpixels add light on black, so overlaps get brighter. Zoom into the CMYK tile to see the rosettes the four screen angles form.
  • Ishihara and the poster grid are the most graphic. On the rainbow sphere, the four-ink poster tile gets close to the Taiwan Spectrum look.

The shader

vec2 cell = floor(gl_FragCoord.xy / uPixel);
vec3 c    = texture2D(tScene, (cell + .5) / uRt).rgb;
float lum = dot(c, vec3(.2126, .7152, .0722));
float t   = rankFromMask(mod(cell, uMaskN));
gl_FragColor = vec4(lum > t ? uLight : uDark, 1.);

Reading the numbers

  • The plain copy column is the floor: a full-screen pass that only samples a texture. The dither pass should sit close to it.
  • Timings ignore vsync and presentation, so they show headroom. A real frame also pays for your own draw calls.
  • Raise the cell size and the scene column drops quickly, because the scene renders at a fraction of the screen.

At 1080p divide by about 4. Rendering the scene at a lower resolution and upscaling with nearest-neighbour filtering cuts the cost further. The frame rate is then limited by the scene and the JS draw calls, not the dithering.

Setup notes

  • Noise texture. Use a pre-baked 64×64 or 128×128 mask with NearestFilter, RepeatWrapping and no mipmaps.
  • Where to run it. A ShaderPass in EffectComposer or a custom full-screen quad. The extra render-target pass usually costs more than the dither itself. Dithering inside one material's fragment shader avoids it.
  • Animation. A static mask looks like a screen door stuck to the glass. Offset it every frame with an R2 or golden-ratio step, or cycle 32–64 pre-baked slices.
  • Linear vs sRGB. Compare against the threshold in linear light, or everything looks darker than intended.
  • Moving cameras. Screen-anchored dither swims over the scene. Anchor it in world or object space as Return of the Obra Dinn does.

Porting notes

How the file is organised

  • Every demo is one <section> and one self-invoking script. Element ids are prefixed per demo: amfm-, shf-, shs-, gg-, cg-, t-.
  • Demos 1 to 5 use Canvas 2D only and have no dependencies. Demo 6 needs WebGL and three.js r128 (the global THREE).
  • The first script defines two shared helpers: segGroup for button groups and watchVisible, which pauses animation when a demo is off screen.
  • All colours come from the tokens at the top of the stylesheet. Demo 6's CSS is scoped under #threejs. Canvases keep a white "paper" ground in both themes.

Reusable cores

  • tone(x, y): the shaded-sphere function used by demos 1 to 4. Swap it to dither any scalar field.
  • ed() / pd(): serpentine error diffusion, grey and palette versions.
  • voidAndCluster(N): the blue-noise mask generator, in the global script of demo 6. It returns a rank per cell.
  • stipples() and tour(): weighted Voronoi stippling and the TSP tour.

Costs and limits

  • Building a 128×128 blue-noise mask took about 1 second in Node and 64×64 about 40 ms. Bake larger masks offline or build them in a worker.
  • The colour gallery's riso and pointillism tiles are the slowest. Tiles render one per animation frame so the page stays responsive.
  • The cost estimates in demo 6 come from how GPUs work. They are not measurements. Use the built-in benchmark for real numbers.
