Hunters and the hunted

Predators and prey have been running an arms race for half a billion years, and many of the moves on both sides turn out to be simple rules: how to search, how to steer, where to stand in a crowd, which way to jump, how to paint your own body. Each section below is a live simulation of one such rule, with the numbers it produces measured as it runs. They make good steering behaviours for game agents, good beats for animation, and they read as alive because real animals use them.

Elements to borrow

A search that looks purposeful

Long straight runs between bursts of local looping read as hunting, not wandering. Lévy search

A chase with style

Pure pursuit tail-chases; proportional navigation cuts across and arrives. Pick the law that fits the character. Pursuit

The hunter who never moves

An approach that holds still against the background until it is very close. Unnerving on screen. Motion camouflage

Crowds that tighten under threat

Everyone pushes inward to put someone else on the edge. Selfish herd

The odd one gets eaten

A predator facing many lookalikes struggles; one different colour is a target painted on. Oddity

A vocabulary of panic

Vacuole, fountain, flash, split, ball: named group shapes you can trigger. Escape repertoire

A two-frame startle

A C-bend, then a snap: the fastest move in the animal kingdom, and the clearest pose for "fright". C-start

Paint the light side dark and the shadow side light and a round body goes flat. Countershading

1 · Viswanathan et al., 1999 · Sims et al., 2008

Lévy search

A forager that cannot see food far away moves in straight runs and turns at random. The question is how long the runs should be. In a Lévy walk run lengths follow a power law, P(ℓ) ∝ ℓ−μ: mostly short steps, with rare very long ones. μ near 3 is ordinary random wandering; μ near 1 is nearly straight-line travel. Viswanathan and colleagues showed that when food is sparse and patches can be revisited, μ ≈ 2 finds the most food per distance travelled. Sims and colleagues found Lévy-like runs in sharks, tuna and turtles where prey is scarce, and ordinary random walks where it is plentiful.

Set μ and watch one forager; the chart measures food found per 1000 units travelled for every μ in the current world. The theory is contested for real animals (Pyke 2015), so read the chart as what this model predicts.

2 · Guidance laws · Brighton et al., 2017

Pursuit laws

Four hunters chase the same prey with the same speed and the same maximum turn rate; only the steering rule differs. Pure pursuit always points at the prey and ends up chasing its tail. Constant bearing heads for the interception point, keeping the line of sight from rotating. Proportional navigation turns N times as fast as the line of sight rotates, like a missile; peregrine falcons do this with N ≈ 3. Harris's hawks mix it with a little pure pursuit (Brighton and Taylor 2019), which copes better with a jinking target at close range.

The falcon's spiral. A falcon's sharpest vision looks out about 40° to the side, not straight ahead. Turning its head to look forward costs drag in a high-speed dive, so it keeps the prey at that fixed angle instead, which puts it on a logarithmic spiral (Tucker 2000). The spiral is longer than the straight line by exactly 1/cos α.

3 · Srinivasan and Davey, 1995 · Mizutani et al., 2003

Motion camouflage

Prey detect a hunter largely by its movement against the background. A hunter can cancel that cue by approaching along a path that keeps it at the same direction as seen from the prey. Two versions: stay on the line between the prey and a fixed landmark, so the hunter sits on top of the landmark in the prey's view; or keep a constant compass direction from the prey, so the hunter matches the infinitely distant background. Male hoverflies and dragonflies do the second during territorial chases. The only remaining cue is looming: the hunter grows.

4 · Hamilton, 1971

The selfish herd

If a predator strikes at a random spot and eats whoever is closest, each animal's risk is the area of its domain of danger: the patch of ground nearer to it than to anyone else (its Voronoi cell). Animals on the edge own huge open domains. So every animal gains by moving toward its neighbours, and a group forms with no one wanting it to: a herd made of selfishness. Which rule animals use to move inward matters; Viscido and colleagues found that heading for the local crowd beats heading for the single nearest neighbour.

Cells are shaded by size, and red crosses are random strikes. Compare the rules by how fast the mean domain shrinks and how much risk stays with whoever started on the outside.

5 · Landeau and Terborgh, 1986 · Ioannou et al., 2008

Confusion and oddity

A predator has to follow one target until it strikes. Many similar animals moving around that target make it hard to keep the lock, so attack success falls as the group grows: the confusion effect. Landeau and Terborgh found largemouth bass caught silvery minnows fast when they were alone and almost never in groups of 15, unless some minnows were dyed blue, in which case the bass caught the blue ones quickly. Being different removes your lookalikes and with them your protection: the oddity effect. This is one reason mixed-species schools sort themselves by size and colour.

Test it on yourself. One fish flashes red, then turns back into the crowd. Follow it with your eyes, and click it when everything stops.

The model behind it. A predator attacks a group spread at constant density. Each attack succeeds with probability 0.9 / (1 + 0.35 k), where k is the number of lookalikes within 4° of the target from where the predator sits. An odd animal has no lookalikes, and the predator picks it half the time. The constants are assumptions; the shapes of the curves are the point.

6 · Pitcher and Parrish, 1993 · Couzin et al., 2002

The escape repertoire

Fish schools answer a predator with a small set of recognisable shapes, catalogued by Pitcher: compaction (close up), vacuole (a hole that travels with the predator), fountain (split ahead, stream past both flanks, rejoin behind), flash expansion (everyone bolts outward in a fraction of a second), split (the school divides into two that rejoin later) and the bait ball (a tight rotating sphere). Each shape here comes from the same schooling model (repulsion, alignment and attraction zones) plus one extra response rule. Drive the shark with your pointer, or let it make passes.

7 · Eaton et al., 2001 · Domenici and Blake, 1997

The C-start

A pair of giant neurons in the fish hindbrain, the Mauthner cells, each connect straight to the muscles of one side of the body. A sudden sound or pressure wave fires one of them, and 5–10 ms later the whole body contracts on one side: stage 1, a C-bend that turns the head away. In stage 2 the fish straightens with a stroke the other way and shoots off. The whole move takes about 30–50 ms. Compare it with an ordinary turn on the right, at the same slowed-down clock. The figures are a kinematic sketch at typical published values for a 10 cm fish.

Unpredictability is a defence. Real escape angles are not fixed: fish choose among a few preferred angles, mostly 90–180° away from the threat, and pick the side. A predator that learns from its misses can beat any fixed escape, but not a varied one. Here a predator aims each lunge at the 30° direction its prey has escaped toward most often so far.

8 · Thayer, 1909 · Kiltie, 1988

Countershading

Light comes from above, so a uniformly coloured round body has a bright back and a dark belly. That gradient is exactly how eyes read three-dimensional shape. Countershading paints the opposite gradient onto the body, darker where it is lit and lighter where it is shaded, so the two cancel and the body looks flat (Thayer's law). The same paint helps from other angles too: a dark back is hard to see from above against the deep, and a pale belly is less obvious from below against the bright surface. Seen from directly below, though, no paint matches the surface light, so some fish and squid make their own light on the belly (counter-illumination). Slide from uniform to fully countershaded.

Notes

For ascii-sharks

Where each idea would slot into the ascii-sharks project, whose fish already school by Couzin zones and already use the fountain effect, flash expansion and a spreading alarm.

Lévy patrol. The shark's "lazy circuit around the headline" in World.step could become runs drawn from a μ ≈ 2 power law between turns when no school is in range, and tight local loops (μ ≈ 3) after a meal.
Proportional navigation. The "aim at where the fish is about to be" pursuit could become turn rate = N × line-of-sight rate with N ≈ 3, clamped by the existing speed-dependent turning circle. It produces the cutting arcs in section 2 without predicting anything.
Oddity and stragglers. The target-selection step ("stick with the current target unless something is clearly better") could add a bonus for fish far from their school's centre or of a minority species, and a lock-loss chance that grows with lookalikes nearby.
C-start. Alarmed fish could hold one frame of C-bend before bolting, with a 5–10 ms equivalent latency, and pick between two or three preferred escape angles at random instead of fleeing straight away.
Countershading. The renderer lights the shark's back from the top-left with flanks falling into shadow. A countershading slider that dims the lit back glyphs and brightens the flank glyphs would let a shark fade into the dark water and pop out when it turns.

Line of sight (LOS)The direction from hunter to prey. Its rotation rate drives proportional navigation.
