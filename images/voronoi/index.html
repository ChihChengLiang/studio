Cells from seeds

Scatter some points and give every spot on the plane to whichever point is closest. The regions you get are a Voronoi diagram, and they turn up wherever things grow outward from seeds and stop when they meet: crystal grains, cracked mud, giraffe patches, plant cells, fish territories. This page grows them, warps them, relaxes them into honeycombs, turns them into stipples, mosaics, textures and shattered glass, and checks the geometry that makes them work as it goes.

1 · Johnson and Mehl, 1939

Growth from seeds

Start a circle growing from every seed at the same moment and the same speed, and stop each one where it touches another. The boundaries are exactly the perpendicular bisectors between seeds: the Voronoi diagram. That is why metal grains, foam-free cell tissue and crusts of drying mud look like Voronoi cells. Real growth is rarely so even. Seeds that start at different times (crystals nucleating as metal cools, Johnson and Mehl's model) meet along hyperbolas. Seeds that grow at different speeds meet along circles. The measurement compares the final map with the plain Voronoi diagram of the same seeds.

2 · Distance

Distance changes the map

"Closest" depends on how you move. As the crow flies is the ordinary (Euclidean) distance. In a grid city you walk along blocks, |dx| + |dy| (Manhattan distance), and the walls tilt to 45°. A king on a chessboard moves diagonally for free, max(|dx|, |dy|) (Chebyshev distance), and the regions turn boxy. John Snow's 1854 cholera map made the same point by hand: which water pump was nearest depended on walking routes, not straight lines. Same 24 seeds in all three; the number under each map is the share of ground that changes hands compared with straight-line distance.

3 · Lloyd, 1982 · Barlow, 1974

Territories and Lloyd relaxation

Male tilapia dig nest pits on the lake floor and defend the ground around them. Each one keeps away from its neighbours, which in effect moves it toward the middle of its own territory, and a crowded breeding ground settles into a near-honeycomb of hexagons (Barlow 1974). The same rule is Lloyd's algorithm: move every seed to the centroid of its cell, recompute, repeat. It converges to a centroidal Voronoi tessellation, the most even way to split a region.

Two pieces of maths come for free. Euler's formula forces the average cell to have six sides, however irregular the pattern. And bigger cells tend to have more sides, which botanists noticed in plant tissue long before (Lewis's law). Cells are coloured by side count: blue for fewer than six, cream for six, orange for more. The right-hand chart is the structure factor of the seed positions: random points are flat at 1; relaxed points have almost no energy at long wavelengths, the signature of blue noise.

4 · Secord, 2002 · Hausner, 2001

Stipples and mosaics

Weight Lloyd's centroids by how dark the picture is and the seeds crowd into the shadows while keeping even spacing: weighted Voronoi stippling, which turns a picture into dots like a pen-and-ink illustrator would. Leave the weights out and colour each cell by the picture underneath to get stained glass. Connect neighbouring seeds instead of drawing their cells and you get the Delaunay triangulation, the base of low-poly art. All three start from the same lit sphere. The stipple measurement compares the blurred dots with the blurred picture, iteration by iteration.

5 · Worley, 1996

Worley textures

Steven Worley's cellular texture keeps not the cell but the distances: F1 to the nearest seed, F2 to the second nearest. F1 looks like rounded cells or bubbles; F2 − F1 is zero exactly on the cell walls, so it draws veins and cracks; combined with a little noise and a colour per cell it becomes stone, scales or leather. Move the seeds slowly and F2 − F1 shimmers like light on the floor of a pool. The seeds sit one per grid cell, so the texture tiles and every lookup only checks nine cells.

6 · Visual effects

Fracture

Effects artists break glass and stone by scattering seeds packed densely around the point of impact and sparsely further out, then cutting the object into their Voronoi cells. The result has many small shards near the hit and a few large ones at the edges, which is roughly what real brittle fracture does. Click the pane to strike it.

7 · Delaunay, 1934

The Delaunay dual

Join every pair of seeds whose cells share a wall and you get a triangulation with a remarkable property: no seed lies inside any triangle's circumscribed circle. Among all ways to triangulate the points, it also makes the smallest angle as large as possible, so its triangles are the least sliver-like, which is why meshes, terrain models and low-poly art use it. The counts are fixed by Euler's formula: with n points, h of them on the hull, there are exactly 2n − 2 − h triangles and 3n − 3 − h edges. Drag points, click empty space to add one, and hover a triangle to see its empty circle.

Glossary and sources

TermMeaning
Voronoi cellAll points closer to one seed than to any other.
BisectorThe line of points equally far from two seeds; Voronoi walls lie on bisectors.
Weighted diagramsAdditive weights (start times) give hyperbolic walls; multiplicative weights (speeds) give circular walls; power diagrams give straight walls that are not bisectors.
Centroidal Voronoi tessellationA diagram in which every seed sits at its cell's centroid; the fixed point of Lloyd's algorithm.
Blue noiseA point set or pattern with little energy at long wavelengths: even spacing without a grid.
Structure factorS(k) = |Σ e^(−ik·x)|² / n: how much a point set varies at wavelength 1/k. Random points give 1.
Lewis's lawIn cell tissue, cell area grows roughly linearly with number of sides.
F1, F2Distance to the nearest and second-nearest seed, the basis of Worley noise.
Delaunay triangulationThe dual of the Voronoi diagram: triangles whose circumcircles contain no other seed.
