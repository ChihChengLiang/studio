What painters know about color

Eight ideas that professionals use daily and most programmers never hear about, each with a live demo, followed by a lab that applies them one at a time to a single image. Use the toolbar above: if a picture fails in Grayscale or Squint, color will not rescue it.

1 · Value structure beats hue

Painters design in grayscale first. Value (how light or dark) decides whether a picture reads; hue is seasoning. If the value structure works, nearly any palette works. If it does not, no palette fixes it.

Below, both scenes use the same five hues. Left: every layer has the same lightness. Right: lightness steps from pale and distant to dark and near. Both look fine in color. Switch the toolbar to Grayscale.

In your repo · Add a grayscale toggle to every demo page (one CSS filter, as on this page). For sprites, check the silhouette in grayscale at 1× before choosing any color.

2 · Color is relative, never absolute

A swatch has no fixed appearance; it takes on the opposite of whatever surrounds it (simultaneous contrast, Albers). Painters judge a color by holding it against its real neighbors, not by naming it.

Every inner square in this demo is exactly the same color. The hex under each row is the same value for all four.

In your repo · When picking palette entries for a sprite or brush layer, judge them on the canvas next to their neighbors. Isolated swatches in a picker mislead.

3 · Shadows shift temperature; they are not darker versions

Multiplying a color toward black produces dead, muddy shading. Real shadows change hue as well: under warm light they go cooler and keep their saturation; highlights go warmer and lose some. The ramp is dark/cool/saturated → light/warm/desaturated. Pixel artists call these hue-shifted ramps.

In your repo · Build the fixed palette in pixel-sprites/render.py as 3–5-step ramps per material with this shift, instead of independent colors. In p5.brush, layer a cool low-opacity glaze in shadow regions.

4 · Chroma is the scarce resource

Pros pick a small palette with one dominant key and spend saturation only where the eye should land (roughly 60/30/10). Muted everything with one vivid area makes that area glow; saturated everywhere reads as noise.

The accent circle is fixed at the same high chroma in every position of the slider.

In your repo · For halftone and dither palettes, give most entries low chroma and reserve one saturated ink. For brush sketches, pick one dominant hue per piece.

5 · Harmony comes from a shared mother color

Colors mixed from the same few pigments relate automatically. Painters push this by adding a touch of one color to everything (a unifying glaze). The hue wheel does the opposite job: it tells you what clashes, not what belongs together.

In your repo · A final pass that adds a_b-offset (shown here) to every pixel is a one-line unifier for any sketch or sprite sheet.

6 · Think in a perceptual space (OKLCH), not HSL or RGB

HSL "lightness" is a lie: yellow at 50% is far brighter than blue at 50%. Perceptual models (Munsell for painters, OKLab/OKLCH for code) make equal numbers look equal. That gives even value steps, and gradients without muddy middles.

Top: twelve hues at HSL 100% / 50%. Bottom: OKLCH L 0.72, C 0.14. Switch to Grayscale: the top row flickers, the bottom is flat.

In your repo · Do error diffusion and palette snapping in OKLab (or at least linear light), not gamma-encoded RGB. Distance in RGB picks the wrong "nearest" color.

7 · Chromatic grays

Painters rarely use neutral gray. They mix near-complements to get a gray that leans warm or cool, and the lean follows the light. Pure desaturation looks lifeless beside it.

Neutral C = 0

Chromatic: cool bias in the darks, warm bias in the lights, C ≤ 0.03. Same lightness steps; in Grayscale mode they are identical.

In your repo · Backgrounds and paper tones for p5.brush sketches: use a tinted off-white, not #ffffff or #808080.

8 · Edges are a contrast budget

Hard, soft and lost edges are chosen on purpose. The focal area gets the sharpest edges and strongest value contrast; everything else is softened or dissolved into its surroundings. There is no separate edge demo here: it is pass 6 of the lab below, where the dark side of the ball is allowed to melt into the wall while the contact point stays crisp.

The same scene (a ball on a table, key light from the upper left) rendered with cumulative changes. Each pass fixes one problem, in the order painters debug: value → temperature → chroma → harmony → edges. Hue was already acceptable at pass 1; it was almost never the problem.

Run the toolbar's Grayscale and Squint on this section. The "loud area" number is the share of pixels with OKLCH chroma above 0.10; "value range" is the lightness gap between the 5th and 95th percentile pixel.

The debug ladder

When a piece feels wrong, change one variable at a time, in this order. Hue is last for a reason.

  1. ValueSquint or grayscale. Is there a clear light, mid, and dark? Is the focal point the highest contrast?
  2. Edges and focusIs detail and sharpness spent only where the eye should go?
  3. TemperatureDo lights and shadows differ in warmth, or is everything one temperature?
  4. ChromaIs saturation concentrated, or spread evenly? Mute everything, then restore one thing.
  5. HueOnly now: swap or nudge hues. Prefer a shared mother tint to a new palette.

Other habits worth copying. Make small, fast thumbnails (3 values) before committing. Do palette-only color studies separate from the drawing. Compare against the reference by flipping or placing side by side; the error is invisible until you reset your eye. Sample colors from the reference rather than naming them: shadows in photos are rarely black, skies rarely pure blue. View it small. Step away.

Good examples to iterate on next

The ideal exercise is small, has a reference to compare against, and has a number you can watch. In order of recommendation:

Recommended

Re-color one sprite (fox or tiger)

Start from render.py's fixed palette and the matching reference image. Passes: silhouette in grayscale, value ramps per material, hue shifts, palette cut to 12–16 colors, mother tint. Metric: mean OKLab distance between your palette and the reference's. Smallest loop, directly reuses existing files.

Alternative

Dither a photo with a painted palette

Same source image, four renderings: RGB nearest, linear-light, OKLab, OKLab with a hue-shifted 5-color palette. Shows the perceptual-space and ramp lessons at once; the metric is visible tone error.

Alternative

p5.brush layered landscape

Section 1's hills, but with watercolor fills, glazing, and a lost-edge treatment on the far ridge. Best for edges and the mother color; hardest to measure.
