Why notes get along

Some pairs of notes sound smooth and others grind. A large part of that is not taste but mechanics: the inner ear splits sound into overlapping frequency bands, and two components that land in the same band beat against each other. Add up that beating over all the overtones of two notes and the familiar intervals fall out, as do new ones for instruments with unusual overtones. Every curve on this page is computed live from a published model, and everything plays. Sound starts only when you press a button; the volume and a stop button sit in the corner.

1 · Physiology

The ear is a filter bank

The basilar membrane in the cochlea is stiff at one end and floppy at the other, so each place along it resonates with a different frequency. The hair cells at each place act like one band-pass filter in a bank of thousands. The width of one such filter is the critical band; Glasberg and Moore measured it as the equivalent rectangular bandwidth, ERB = 24.7 × (4.37 f/kHz + 1) Hz. That is about 50 Hz wide at 200 Hz and about 130 Hz at 1 kHz, so the bank is roughly evenly spaced on a log-like scale, which is how the x-axis below is drawn.

Two pure tones far apart excite different filters and are heard as two notes. Two tones within one band excite the same filters, and that filter's output swells and fades at the difference frequency. A few hertz apart you hear slow beats; from roughly 15 Hz up to a fraction of the band the beats blur into roughness; past the band, the tones separate and the roughness goes away.

2 · Plomp and Levelt, 1965

The roughness curve

Plomp and Levelt played pairs of pure tones to listeners and asked how pleasant each pair was. The answer depended only on how far apart the tones were measured in critical bands: smooth at unison, roughest at about a quarter of a band, smooth again past one band. Sethares fitted that curve with two exponentials, and this page uses his fit throughout.

Plotted against musical interval, the curves for different registers look different: the roughest interval in the bass is several semitones wide, and in the treble it is less than one. Switch the x-axis to critical bands and the five curves collapse into one. That collapse is the whole claim of the theory.

3 · Sethares, 1993

Overtones make the intervals

Real notes are not pure tones. A string or a voice sounds a fundamental plus overtones at 2, 3, 4… times its frequency. To score an interval between two such notes, add up the roughness of every pair of overtones, one from each note. The total dips wherever the two sets of overtones line up, because coinciding overtones do not beat. With harmonic overtones those dips land on the ratios 6:5, 5:4, 4:3, 3:2, 5:3 and 2:1: the minor and major thirds, the fourth, the fifth, the major sixth and the octave. With seven or more overtones, shallower dips at 7:6, 7:5 and 7:4 appear too: "blue" intervals that standard tuning misses by about 30 cents.

Start with one overtone (a pure tone): the curve has no dips at all. Add overtones one at a time and watch each dip appear. The lower plot shows the two notes' overtones and the pairs that are beating at the interval under the cursor.

4 · Tuning, timbre, spectrum, scale

Every timbre has its own scale

If smooth intervals come from overtones lining up, then an instrument with different overtones has different smooth intervals. A struck metal bar has overtones at 2.76, 5.40 and 8.93 times its fundamental (the modes of a free bar), not 2, 3 and 4. A stretched tone, whose "octave" overtone sits at 2.1 instead of 2, has its whole set of dips stretched with it.

Sethares' best-known demonstration follows. Take a stretched timbre and play an ordinary cadence in standard tuning: it sounds sour. Stretch the tuning by the same amount and it sounds in tune again, even though no interval in it is a "correct" one any more. Pick a preset or drag the overtones (the fundamental is fixed), then play the cadence both ways. The roughness numbers under the buttons are computed, not quoted.

5 · Harmony as a curve

Tension and release

Sum the roughness over every pair of notes in a chord and you get one number per chord. Run it along a progression and you get a tension curve, the musical twin of a story's intensity curve. Two things show up immediately: added and clashing notes raise it, and the same chord gets rougher as it moves down, because the critical bands are wider in the bass. That is the physical reason arrangers spread chords out below and pack them close above.

Numbers are in units of one semitone at middle C (C4 with C♯4 = 1.00), with a six-overtone harmonic timbre. Click keys to build a chord; choose a progression to see and hear its curve.

6 · Virtual pitch

The missing fundamental

Take a 220 Hz tone and delete the 220 Hz component. You still hear 220 Hz. The overtones that remain (440, 660, 880…) all repeat together every 4.55 ms, and the auditory system reads pitch from that shared period, not from the lowest component. Terhardt called this virtual pitch. It is why a phone speaker that cannot reproduce 100 Hz still plays a bass line you can hum.

The last option shifts the overtones up by 40 Hz so they are no longer a harmonic series. The waveform no longer repeats exactly, and the pitch you hear moves by only a few hertz, not by 40: one of the classic experiments that ruled out "pitch = lowest frequency" (Schouten, de Boer). 7 · Shepard, 1964 · Risset

A staircase with no top

Build a tone from components spaced exactly an octave apart and weight them with a fixed bell curve over log frequency. Raise every component a semitone. After twelve steps each component sits where the one above it started, so the sound is identical to where it began, yet every step sounded like a step up. The components leaving at the top are inaudibly quiet, and new ones fade in at the bottom. Pitch has two parts, height and chroma (which note of the octave), and this sound has chroma without a fixed height. Risset's glide version slides continuously. It is the sound version of the Shepard gradient in the halftone notebook.

Glossary and sources

TermMeaning
Partial / overtoneOne sine component of a sound. The fundamental is the first partial.
HarmonicPartials at whole-number multiples of the fundamental, as in strings, winds and voices.
Critical bandThe bandwidth of one auditory filter. Tones within one band interact; tones further apart are heard separately.
ERBEquivalent rectangular bandwidth, Glasberg and Moore's measurement of the critical band: 24.7 × (4.37 f/kHz + 1) Hz.
BeatsSlow loudness fluctuation at the difference frequency of two close tones.
RoughnessThe buzzy sensation when beats are too fast to follow, from roughly 15 Hz to a fraction of a critical band.
Sensory consonanceSmoothness explained by roughness alone. Harmonicity (how much a chord resembles one harmonic tone), familiarity and context also shape what sounds consonant; this page models only the first.
Dissonance curveTotal roughness of two notes as a function of the interval between them. Its dips are candidate scale steps.
Cent1/100 of an equal-tempered semitone; 1200 cents to the octave.
Pseudo-octaveThe interval that plays the octave's role for a non-harmonic timbre, such as 2.1:1 for a stretched tone.
Virtual pitchPitch heard from the common period of the partials, even with the fundamental absent.
Chroma and heightThe two parts of pitch: which note of the octave, and how high. Shepard tones keep chroma and blur height.
